Extraction is the easy half

Parvez Ahmed · Numbers from the held-out re-run of 18 July 2026, 100 documents the pipeline had never seen.

Getting a model to read a bank statement takes an afternoon. Knowing whether it read the statement correctly is the actual engineering problem, and it is the one almost nobody demos.

DocVal takes a bank statement — a native PDF or a photographed scan — and returns typed, structured transactions. Then it does the part that matters: it checks the arithmetic. Opening balance plus every credit minus every debit must equal the closing balance. The running-balance chain has to hold row by row. Dates have to fall inside the statement period and stay in order. If the maths doesn't hold, the extraction doesn't ship clean — it ships flagged.

That check is possible because a bank statement is one of the rare documents that carries its own answer key. The numbers on it have to add up. So instead of asking a second model whether the first model did well, you can just do the sum.

How a document moves through it

1 · Triage Does the PDF have a real text layer, or is it pixels?
2 · Extract Native PDFs go through a deterministic pdfplumber parser. Scans go to Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite vision, per page.
3 · Schema Both paths land in one Pydantic model. Money is Decimal, never float.
4 · Validate Balance continuity, running-balance chain, date sanity. Pure functions, no model in the loop.

The vision model returns a wire schema of plain strings, and normalisation into canonical types is ordinary deterministic code. That split is deliberate: models copy what is printed on the page no matter how firmly you ask them not to, so $ becomes USD in Python, with a test, rather than in a prompt.

The number, and the number underneath it

Held-out transaction F1 is 0.63. On its own that is a mediocre number and it tells you almost nothing. Split by where the documents came from, it tells you everything:

Flash-Lite reads clean synthetic pages almost perfectly and garbles dense real statements — rows shifted across columns, debit and credit swapped, digits misread. The validation layer flags exactly those documents rather than accepting them quietly, which is why the validation pass rate is a deeply unglamorous 0.25. A low pass rate on hard documents is the system working. A high one would mean the checks were too weak to notice.

The fix that moved it

The first held-out run scored 0.12 on real scans with a 13% error rate. The eval told me why: 10 of 13 failures were the model running through a 32k output-token cap on dense multi-page documents, collapsing or dropping rows. That is not "bad extraction" — it is a structural failure with a structural fix. Dense documents now get extracted a page at a time and merged, with the running-balance validator checking the seams.

The cost line is there on purpose. An accuracy gain that multiplies your bill by ten is a different decision from a free one, and you cannot make that call if you only ever measured accuracy. Mean latency is 70.9 seconds per document on the vision path; native-text PDFs cost nothing and return immediately.

What the data itself got wrong

The validation layer earned its place before the pipeline was even finished. Running it over the primary corpus surfaced that roughly 12% of transaction rows in the AgamiAI dataset have internally inconsistent running balances — not from a bad extraction, but in the published ground-truth JSON and in the rendered PDFs themselves. The source data does not add up.

You only ever find that by checking. A pipeline that trusts its inputs would have quietly scored itself against wrong answers and reported a number nobody could have challenged.
