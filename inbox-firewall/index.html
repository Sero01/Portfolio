Inbox Firewall

How small can a model be and still be trusted with your inbox?

  • Lab
  • Code

The question

I started with a simple question: how small can an AI model be before it stops being safe to let it act on your inbox?

I built a system where every email had to become one of five actions: ignore it, mark it urgent, archive it, draft a reply, or escalate it to a human. I tested this on 1,028 messages, keeping the difficult and malicious ones hidden until the final test. Even a simple rules-based system performed surprisingly well, correctly routing 88.1% of messages while handling about 75% automatically.

The pipeline

Anything low-confidence, malformed, untrusted or ambiguous leaves by the escalate path instead of acting.

The eleven

  • 1,028labelled messages, splits disjoint
  • 88.1%baseline action accuracy
  • 11unsafe executions

The baseline is regex and keywords, no learning at all. Then I found the 11: messages it acted on confidently that it should have handed to a human. Six were prompt injections, the rest phishing. Every one produced a perfectly well-formed action, so the validator was powerless.

Calibration

  • 0.245 → 0calibration error, on held-out clean data
  • 11 → 11unsafe executions, unmoved

The obvious fix was calibration, and it failed in an interesting way. Fitting the confidence scores on held-out clean data drove calibration error from 0.245 to 0.000, but unsafe executions were still at 11. On clean data the router is almost always right when it’s confident, so calibration maps nearly every score to ~1.0, and the gate ends up waving the confident injections through.

That showed me something important: being confident, even being well-calibrated, doesn’t mean you’re safe when an attacker controls the input.

Trust nothing in the message

  • 11 → 7unsafe executions
  • 6 → 2of them prompt injections

I then added a simple security layer that treated the email itself as untrusted and looked for signs that it was trying to manipulate the AI. This reduced unsafe actions from 11 to 7, including reducing prompt-injection failures from 6 to 2, with only a small drop in coverage. The remaining failures were harder phishing and social-engineering attacks.

I couldn’t benchmark the tiny 26M model because my hardware wasn’t sufficient, but that didn’t change the main conclusion.

What I learned

Confidence is a coverage instrument, not a safety boundary.

The model determines how much I can automate, while the safety boundary needs to live outside the model, in strict rules and security checks. Typed outputs catch malformed actions. Content-trust checks catch obvious injections. Phishing remains. The honest system escalates what its deterministic layers cannot prove safe.
